Explainable Transaction Risk Monitoring for Fintech

Python · Tableau · Fraud Analytics Explainability · Risk Ops Behavioral Finance · FinTech
Problem What I built Dashboard Key findings Methodology

In transaction monitoring, there is always a temptation to flag as many signals as possible. The result is a large review queue where real risk becomes harder, not easier, to read.

In this project, I built an explainable risk layer on top of a public fraud dataset so that each flagged case carried not only a score, but also a severity level, a primary trigger, and a short explanation for escalation.

The goal was not to add one more score. The goal was to make risk readable: where it appears, how the system escalates it, and what actually lands in review first.

What I built

I used a transaction-level fraud dataset to simulate a monitoring workflow with nearly 284 thousand transactions, 473 fraud cases, a dedicated high-risk segment, and a separate critical escalation layer. [file:326]

In Python, I built a Combined Risk Score that brought together several interpretable signals: unusual amount, off-hours activity, model-based risk, and anomaly features. On top of that, I created severity buckets, alert flags, primary risk triggers, and short reason-for-flag explanations for flagged transactions.

Risk logic

Transaction-level preparation, risk indicators, Combined Risk Score, severity buckets, and explainability fields.

Dashboard

KPI overview, fraud rate by severity, alert time patterns, and an investigator queue in one workspace.

Queue

A queue structured by priority, severity, trigger, and reason for flag, designed for review rather than raw listing.

What the dashboard shows

The dashboard is structured as a single working screen. The top section gives a high-level view of the flow, the middle focuses on alert quality by severity, and the lower part moves into time patterns and the investigator queue.

The Tableau dashboard is embedded directly into the page, allowing the reader to move from top-level KPIs to specific transactions in the queue.

The top layer shows the basic picture: 283,726 transactions, 473 fraud cases, a 0.17% fraud rate, 970 high-risk transactions, and 142 critical transactions. This immediately shows that the zone of elevated attention is only a very small part of the total flow. [file:326]

The key analytical block is fraud rate by severity. This is where it becomes clear which segments truly concentrate fraud and which ones generate alert volume without proportional value. The queue below translates that logic into operations: severity, priority, trigger, and the reason each case was escalated.

Here, the dashboard is not a decorative layer. It is a short route from signal to action: what the system escalates, why it does so, and what the reviewer actually sees.

Key findings

At the full-flow level, the fraud rate is low: 0.17%, or 473 cases. But risk is distributed very unevenly. The high-risk segment covers only 970 transactions, and the critical segment only 142. That means the core challenge is not to review everything, but to narrow manual attention to the right part of the stream. [file:326]

The strongest operating segment was High / P2. It remained small in volume while concentrating a meaningful share of fraud cases. From a review perspective, this is exactly what a useful monitoring layer should produce: a segment narrow enough for manual handling, but dense enough to justify attention.

Critical / P1 behaved differently. It is the loudest escalation zone, but its internal precision appears lower than expected for the highest priority tier. Some critical alerts end in non-fraud outcomes, which makes this segment look less like the system’s best catch and more like a candidate for threshold adjustment.

Trigger analysis also shows a clear difference between strong and weak signals. Model-driven risk produces a better concentration of fraud, while simpler signals such as unusual amount or off-hours activity can generate substantial alert volume without the same level of quality. In practice, that means some rules work more like noise generators unless they are combined with stronger signals.

Time patterns add another layer of interpretation. Night segments carry lower transaction volume but a higher likelihood of suspicious behavior. Still, the off-hours signal on its own is too broad. It becomes more useful when it amplifies an already risky context rather than acting as a standalone reason for escalation.

Overall, this case is not just about the presence of fraud. It is about how a monitoring system allocates attention. High looks like a strong operating segment, Medium can function as a buffer for additional review, and Critical in its current form looks like the most likely candidate for retuning.

Technical details

Case type

A portfolio simulation of fraud monitoring on a public transaction-level dataset, built around a custom risk logic, severity segmentation, and an investigator queue.

Python work

  • Cleaning and preparing transaction-level data.
  • Calculating risk indicators: unusual amount, off-hours activity, amount-/time-based risk, model-based signals, and anomaly components.
  • Building a Combined Risk Score and segmenting transactions into Low / Medium / High / Critical.
  • Creating alert flags, primary risk triggers, and short text explanations for flagged transactions.
  • Calculating core KPIs: total flow, fraud rate, high-risk volume, and critical volume. [file:326]

Tableau work

  • High-level KPI overview for fast flow assessment.
  • Fraud rate by severity to evaluate segment quality.
  • Alert time patterns.
  • An investigator queue with priority, severity, trigger, and explanation fields.

Tech stack

  • Python
  • VS Code
  • Tableau Public
