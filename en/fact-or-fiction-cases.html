TELWHA · Fact or Fiction

20 financial myths about money

Every statement here is built on my own mini cases: I worked with current, real, or carefully simulated data, created the charts, and translated the numbers into clear behavioral logic.

Fact or fiction?

The explanation opens right below each question, together with a short takeaway and the behavioral trap behind it.

Debt, income, savings

1–5
Question 1Fact or fiction?

Unsecured debt is mainly a problem only for low-income households.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Debt is not the same as poverty

In this case, based on Institute for Fiscal Studies data, a meaningful share of unsecured debt appears among households with above-average income. In other words, debt is not only a story about financial shortage, but also about liquidity, financing style, and behavioral motives.

Trap: a class stereotype — treating debt as the same thing as chronic poverty.
Unsecured debt is also present among higher-income households.
Question 2Fact or fiction?

If a person has money in the bank, they will always pay off expensive debt immediately.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

A buffer often beats arithmetic

Cases on savings, credit debt, and behavioral motives show that people often keep debt and reserves at the same time. Mathematically, paying off the debt may be better, but psychologically it matters not to lose the feeling of backup and control.

Trap: mental accounting and the price of a calm night’s sleep.
Having assets to repay debt does not mean debt will be paid off right away.
Question 3Fact or fiction?

Anyone with an emergency fund is already handling money wisely.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

A reserve does not cancel bias

An emergency fund often reduces financial fragility and increases the feeling of control. But it does not automatically make someone rational: anxiety, delay, or impulsive reactions may still remain.

Trap: confusing the presence of resources with the absence of behavioral errors.
A reserve lowers fragility, but it does not remove behavioral bias.
Question 4Fact or fiction?

If a household has enough financial assets, it will not carry unsecured debt.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Assets do not guarantee the “ideal” choice

In this case, based on reported findings, more than half of households with unsecured debt had enough financial assets to repay it. But people do not always act according to the formula “I see debt, so I close debt”: liquidity logic, fear of losing a buffer, and the habit of keeping money in separate “pockets” all matter too.

Trap: the rationalist illusion — assuming people automatically choose the mathematically best option.
Some households have enough assets to repay debt but do not do so.
Question 5Fact or fiction?

Debt is mostly about poverty: once income rises, debt disappears.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

As income grows, debt changes — it does not vanish

Cases on income and debt show that rising income does not automatically erase debt. What changes is its structure, purpose, and context: from covering shortage to acting as a liquidity tool, a consumption tool, or a way to finance large purchases.

Trap: linear thinking — as if wealthier people automatically live debt-free.
Debt does not automatically disappear as income or net worth grows.

Payment methods and digital money

6–10
Question 6Fact or fiction?

Whether a person pays with cash, card, or phone barely affects how much they spend.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

The payment method changes how spending feels

In the cashless effect case, the lowest spending appeared with cash, higher spending with cards, and even higher spending with phones. The less tangible the moment of payment feels, the easier it becomes to spend.

Trap: the illusion that payment methods are neutral and do not shape behavior.
Cash, card, and phone affect spending differently.
Question 7Fact or fiction?

Mobile payments do not change behavior — they are just a more convenient version of card payments.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Less friction makes spending easier

In the mobile payments case, they produced higher spending than card payments. The less “real” the moment of parting with money feels, the easier it is to move beyond the original plan.

Trap: underestimating the role of small frictions that keep spending in check.
Mobile payments weaken the feeling of spending even more.
Question 8Fact or fiction?

Buy now, pay later is just a technical payment format, not a behavioral trap.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Convenience can push decisions too

In the buy-now-pay-later case, users had higher average order sizes and larger revolving debt balances. When payment is shifted into the future, the burden feels lighter in the moment, and saying yes becomes easier.

Trap: present bias — underestimating future burden in exchange for convenience now.
BNPL is linked to bigger baskets and higher debt balances.
Question 9Fact or fiction?

If a country already has internet and smartphones, almost everyone will use digital finance.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Access is not the same as habit

Cases on digital finance show that internet access alone does not guarantee active use of financial services. Trust, institutions, habits, formal financial inclusion, and country context all shape actual behavior.

Trap: technological blindness — assuming access is a sufficient condition for behavior.
Internet access is linked to digital finance, but it does not guarantee mass adoption.
Question 10Fact or fiction?

People in richer countries rely less on borrowing because they can afford to live without debt.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Wealth does not mean a life without borrowing

Cases based on international data show that rising prosperity does not remove debt — it changes its form. Higher income often means not the absence of borrowing, but a different type of credit activity.

Trap: assuming financial capacity automatically leads to debt-free living.
As prosperity grows, the form of debt changes more than its existence.

Strange patterns that are easy to believe

11–13
Question 11Fact or fiction?

The more Nicolas Cage films are released, the more people drown in swimming pools.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

A beautiful correlation can still be nonsense

In this case, the two lines move almost together, but there is no meaningful real-world connection between them. It is a classic example of how numbers can look convincing even when the story behind them is absurd.

Trap: seeing a neat pattern and automatically inventing cause and effect.
A strong correlation still does not mean a real relationship.
Question 12Fact or fiction?

Margarine consumption can explain the divorce rate in a US state.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Not everything that fits beautifully on a chart makes sense

In this case, the lines look as if margarine really does “damage marriages.” But in reality, it is just a strong accidental correlation, not evidence of causation — and that is exactly why the example is so useful.

Trap: falling in love with a beautiful line on a chart without asking whether there is a real mechanism behind it.
Even an almost perfect correlation can be false.
Question 13Fact or fiction?

Miss America’s age is linked to unusual deaths caused by steam and hot objects.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Numbers sometimes invent stories better than people do

In this case too, the indicators are correlated in a way that looks surprisingly serious. But any causal explanation here sounds like fiction, not science — and that is exactly what makes the example valuable.

Trap: treating any data-driven story as true if it looks persuasive on a chart.
A polished chart can create the illusion of meaning where none exists.

Bias, choice, self-control

14–20
Question 14Fact or fiction?

People mostly make money mistakes because they do not have enough information.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

The problem is often behavior, not knowledge

My cases show that people make money mistakes not only because of limited information, but because of delay, overconfidence, loss aversion, social comparison, and other biases. Knowing what is “correct” does not automatically mean doing it.

Trap: reducing complex behavior to a simple information gap.
Behavioral biases are often stronger than knowledge itself.
Question 15Fact or fiction?

If you clearly explain what a person should do with money, they will simply start doing it.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Understanding does not guarantee action

Cases on choice architecture show that even when people are given a clear explanation, they do not rush to change their behavior. Status quo bias, delay, fear of mistakes, and extra steps can all be stronger than a good instruction.

Trap: rationalist optimism — assuming knowledge automatically turns into action.
Even the right action is often postponed unless the choice architecture changes.
Question 16Fact or fiction?

To make people save more, it is enough to give them the right option — rationality will do the rest.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Defaults are often stronger than intentions

In the auto-enrolment case, participation increased sharply not when people were simply offered options, but when the default scenario itself changed. People often stay with what the system sets for them by default.

Trap: underestimating the power of status quo and default choice.
A default option can sharply change savings participation.
Question 17Fact or fiction?

If there is an option to save more for retirement, people will actively choose it on their own.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

The right option does not always lead to active choice

In the retirement saving case, contribution growth happened because of automatic steps and soft design choices, not because people were actively switching to the better option at scale. The existence of a good option alone does not change behavior.

Trap: overestimating active decision-making and underestimating inertia.
The mere existence of a good option does not guarantee people will choose it.
Question 18Fact or fiction?

In finance, the more self-confidence a person has, the better.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Overconfidence can also do harm

In the financial confidence case, the strongest outcome was not extreme confidence, but a balanced level of confidence. Too little confidence can paralyze action, but too much can push people to overestimate their own ability.

Trap: treating confidence as a pure asset rather than a double-edged tool.
Balanced confidence works better than excessive confidence.
Question 19Fact or fiction?

One general “money pot” works just as well as keeping several separate “envelopes.”

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Envelope logic really changes behavior

In the case comparing one shared account with separate envelopes, people with mentally or practically divided money saved differently and spent differently. Formally the money is the same, but the way it is psychologically organized changes decisions.

Trap: ignoring mental accounting and assuming all money feels the same.
Mental separation of money changes spending and saving behavior.
Question 20Fact or fiction?

If a person is extremely frugal and constantly restricts themselves, that almost always means financial maturity.

Correct answer: fiction
What my case shows

Sometimes “frugality” is anxiety, not stability

Cases about scarcity thinking show that strict self-denial does not always signal healthy financial maturity. Often it reflects not strategy, but a persistent fear of shortage and an inner feeling that safety exists only when almost nothing is allowed.

Trap: confusing carefulness with living in a permanent internal scarcity mode.
Strict self-restraint may come from anxiety rather than a mature strategy.
